Prior to the turn of the Anthropocene, scientist, Mary Somerville, extensively documented how the photosensitive material of plants reacts to ultraviolet light and presented her findings to scientist, Sir John Herschel. Herschel proceeded to test her findings with the expectation of inventing color photography via the harnessing of plants and their organic properties, a pursuit he ultimately abandoned. However, in 1842, these tests resulted in the anthotype, a naturally pigmented form of contact printing; a method defined by its slowness and dependence on botanical materials, in lieu of metallic salts. Exposure time ranges from hours to months to reveal the direct positive image within the plant-based emulsion.

Contemporary anthotypes are understood as a form of sustainable art, an opportunity to examine the effects of time on an environment and how light can create “a deconstruction of the larger landscape, allowing for contemplation of the individual parts.” (Jennifer Linnea Daly, “The Temporal Nature of Things”) These individual parts are affected by pollution, harvesting points, pesticides, additives, in addition to the unique ways each plant will respond to light. By reacting to the state, or more specifically, the health of a natural environment, anthotypes can be a vessel for communication between the photographer and the land. These variables manifest via factors like color, scent, visual contrast, among other tactile and visual characteristics which affect the final image. This is the transformation of a photographer's role, evolving from one disconnected from the natural world, to one where it is a necessity to consider the state of the environment. The aesthetic variations which can occur even within the same plant species contribute to an understanding of land which grants attention to individual components, rather than viewing natural landscapes as impenetrable masses of wilderness.

I make anthotypes (mostly) from either turmeric powder or the brine of red cabbages. Red cabbages make a blue pigment when they oxidize and it is, in my opinion, the easiest emulsion to use. You can mix baking soda and water to make a DIY developer to make greenish pigments as well. The prints only need a day in the sun. I made my first anthotypes in 2023 and they look much different now.
