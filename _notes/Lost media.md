Ziggy Cashmere (a lovely alias) wrote an amazing guide on finding lost media. It is free and online [here](https://sites.google.com/view/lostmediahandbook/lost-media-and-research-handbook). 

Since graduating, I been have been browsing the [lost media wiki](https://lostmediawiki.com/Home) when I have the time. I am specifically interested in finding [missing animated shorts](https://lostmediawiki.com/Astrology_with_Squidward_(found_series_of_%22SpongeBob_SquarePants%22_Nickelodeon_animated_shorts;_early_2000s)) and the [lost English dubs of anime series](https://lostmediawiki.com/One_Piece_(found_4Kids_English_dub_of_anime;_2004-2006)). As someone who researches film, it serves me well to know how to find media which has not been publicized on popular channels, so thanks again to Mr. Cashmere. Also, as someone who thinks about the materiality of film, I am interested in finding the traces of nitrate film published online. Nitrate film is highly flammable and due to this condition, it has a propensity to combust. Thus, roughly 75% of American silent films have been lost forever, [according to the American Film Institute](https://www.afi.com/news/afi-catalog-spotlight-the-recent-discovery-of-a-lost-silent-film/).

Film decays and becomes brittle. The emulsion bubbles until the image appears as if it has [rotten](https://www.filmcare.org/vd_binder.php). This is accepted and accredited to the fragility of physical objects. However, I believe we have to be prepared for the [inevitable decay of digital media](https://www.getty.edu/news/preventing-digital-decay/).
